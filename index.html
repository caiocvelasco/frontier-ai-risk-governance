FRONTIER AI RISK GOVERNANCE · POLICY CONCEPT

From Safety Evaluations to Policy Action

How should European policymakers use frontier AI safety evaluations when deciding whether additional oversight or safeguards are warranted?

Safety evaluations can reveal dangerous AI capabilities, but they do not tell us everything about real-world risks. This project explores how policymakers can interpret these results, understand their limitations, and use them to make better decisions.

THE POLICY PROBLEM

An evaluation result is evidence about a model under specified conditions - not a guarantee of safety in deployment.

Public policy needs a defensible way to move from what was tested, to what the evidence supports, to what an authority might do next. The proposed contribution is a concise policy analysis of that translation, not a new benchmark, certification system, or claim that existing EU rules lack evaluation requirements.

Policy case: Evaluating dangerous cyber capabilities

Hypothetical illustration, not a real model assessment. Consider a frontier model whose developer reports that it passed internal safety evaluations relating to potentially dangerous cyber capabilities. How useful is that statement to an authority considering systemic risks?

ILLUSTRATIVE CLAIM

“Our frontier AI model passed its safety evaluations.”

What would an EU decision-maker need to know before relying on that claim?

01 · EVIDENCE What was actually tested? Which cyber-related capabilities were evaluated? Were tests adversarial and independently scrutinized? What tasks, access, safeguards, and model versions were covered? What failures or limits were disclosed? “Passed” is not meaningful without a documented scope and criteria.
02 · INFERENCE What can we conclude? How closely do test conditions resemble plausible misuse or deployment scenarios? Could access to tools, user adaptation, or later model changes alter the risk? Which uncertainties remain? Capability findings do not directly quantify real-world likelihood or harm.
03 · POLICY What action is justified? Depending on the evidence, should the relevant authority seek further documentation, examine mitigation measures, request additional evaluation, or consider other oversight steps within its legal powers? Responses should reflect risk, uncertainty, proportionality, and legal authority.


Why this matters in Europe:

  • The EU AI Act, Article 55, already requires providers of general-purpose AI models with systemic risk to evaluate their models, assess and mitigate risks, report serious incidents, and ensure appropriate cybersecurity protection.

But an important question remains: How can evidence from AI safety evaluations better support the implementation and supervision of these requirements?

Potential policy output:

  • A short policy brief examining what current safety evaluations reveal, what information may be missing, and how European policymakers could use this evidence to improve oversight.

A framework for policy-relevant evidence

Three questions connect AI safety evidence to accountable European governance. The framework is deliberately decision-oriented rather than a comprehensive technical evaluation methodology.

01EVIDENCE QUALITY

Can we assess the evaluation claim?

Examine test scope, methods, provenance, independence, disclosed limitations, and whether a claim is supported by the underlying evidence.

Proposed approach

A bounded desk-based policy project using public sources. Any empirical comparison would depend on available, sufficiently comparable material.

STEP 01

Map existing duties

Summarize relevant EU requirements and guidance for general-purpose AI models with systemic risk, focusing on evaluation, risk management, and oversight.

STEP 02

Review public evidence

Compare a small, clearly selected set of public safety evaluation documents. Record what is reported, what remains unknown, and which claims can be supported.

STEP 03

Develop policy options

Identify practical improvements to decision-relevant reporting or scrutiny; distinguish feasible proposals from legal duties and note trade-offs.

↗

Scope and research integrity

This is a proposed policy exercise. No model testing, independent audit, interviews, original data collection, or empirical validation has been completed. A cyber-capability test does not by itself establish the probability of harm. Examples are hypothetical; recommendations would require source review and attention to confidentiality, legal powers, and institutional feasibility.

Potential Policy Contribution: a European policy brief

PROPOSED DELIVERABLE

Working title: Making Frontier AI Safety Evaluations Decision-Useful for European Policymakers

A concise policy brief would examine how public reporting on frontier AI evaluations can better support oversight of models with potential systemic risks. It would explain a specific evidence-to-decision gap, assess relevant European institutional and legal context, and propose a limited number of actionable improvements-with trade-offs and uncertainties made explicit.

A small evidence matrix could support the brief by tracing each documented claim to its source, limitations, and policy relevance. The brief - not a software platform or a completed research study - would be the principal proposed output.
